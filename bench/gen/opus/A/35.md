# Write It Down Before You Build It

*Daniel Reyes, Staff Engineer, Thornbury Labs*

## The meeting that should have been a page

Last spring, six of us spent an hour in a room arguing about a caching layer. By the end we had agreed on a plan, and two weeks later we found out we had agreed on three different plans. Everyone left with a picture in their head. Nobody left with the same picture, and nothing written down could show us the difference.

A single page would have caught it. Not because writing is magic, but because a page holds still. You can point at a sentence and say "that's not what I meant." You can't point at a memory of a meeting. Since then I have come to believe that a short written design note, done before the work starts, saves more time than it costs, and by a wide margin.

## What a design note is

A design note is one to two pages, written before any code exists, and read asynchronously. That's the whole definition, and each part of it matters.

One to two pages, because length is a cost to the reader, and a note nobody finishes changes nothing. Before code, because the point is to change course while changing course is cheap. Once a branch exists, people defend it. Read asynchronously, because the useful reactions are rarely the quick ones. A reviewer who reads the note on Tuesday morning with a coffee will notice the missing failure mode. The same reviewer, put on the spot in a meeting, will nod.

The note states the problem, the proposed approach, and the alternatives that were weighed and set aside. It says what the author is unsure about. That last part is the most valuable and the one people most often leave out.

> The page is where you find out what you do not know.

Most of us start writing believing we understand the problem. Then we reach the paragraph where we have to explain how the old records get migrated, and the sentence won't come. That stall is the note doing its job. Better to stall there than in week three of the build.

## What it is not

A design note is not a specification. It doesn't list every field, endpoint and error code. Those details belong in the code and its tests, where they can be checked. A note that tries to be a spec grows to fifteen pages, goes stale before it is merged, and teaches the team that writing is a chore.

It is also not a permission slip. Nobody needs a stamp from a committee to start work, and the note is not a gate. It is a way of thinking in public. If you share it and nobody objects, go build. If someone objects, you have just learned something before it got expensive.

## The cost

Writing takes time, and I won't pretend otherwise. At Thornbury we looked back at the design notes written this year. The median one took 3 hours to write. Some took longer, and many took less.

Against that, the team counted 11 projects in 2026 where a note caught a wrong assumption before any code was written. In one, a service we planned to call didn't expose the data we needed. In another, two teams were each about to build the same importer. None of these were subtle bugs. They were the kind of mistake that is obvious once written down and invisible until then.

I don't have a precise figure for the time those catches saved, and I'd distrust anyone who claimed one. But one wrong assumption that survives into a build routinely costs a team days or weeks. Set that against three hours of writing, and the comparison isn't close.

## Start small

You don't need a template committee or a new review process. Next time you are about to start something that will take more than a few days, open a blank document and give yourself an hour. Write until you hit the sentence you can't finish. Then share it with the two people most likely to disagree with you.

Every note, however short, should answer four questions:

- **What problem** are we solving?
- **Why now** rather than later?
- **What we considered**, including the options we rejected?
- **How we will know it worked**?
