What Eighteen Months of Four-Day Weeks Did to Our Support Team

PA Priscilla Adeyemi-KohHead of Customer Support

On 2 October 2026 we made it official: the support team at Halden Cartography will keep working four-day weeks. That decision closed a pilot that began on 7 April 2025, almost eighteen months earlier. I want to write down what we learned while it is fresh, including the parts that did not go smoothly. When we started, I went looking for honest accounts from other support teams and found mostly cheerleading or mostly warnings. This is ours, as plainly as I can tell it.

Why we tried it

There are 23 people on our support team. In 2024, our annual attrition was 21 percent, and when we asked people on their way out why they were leaving, burnout was the reason they gave most often.

That stung. Support is a craft that takes a long time to learn: our products, our customers, the question behind the question. Every departure took some of that knowledge out the door, and every replacement meant months of the rest of the team carrying extra weight while someone new found their feet.

I could have reached for smaller fixes, and I did wonder whether I was overreacting. But the exit conversations kept circling the same thing: people did not get enough time away from the queue to recover. A four-day week went straight at that. It was also something we could measure, which mattered to me as much as the idea itself.

How the schedule works

The core is simple. Everyone works Monday to Thursday, nine hours a day, for 36 hours a week at full pay. Nobody took a pay cut; that was settled before anything else.

The harder question was Friday. Our customers still need help at the end of the week, so Friday coverage comes from a rotating on-call group of four people. Whoever is on the Friday rotation takes Monday off instead, so they still get three days away, just shifted.

I like this design because it keeps the whole team together four days a week, which makes collaboration easy, and it confines the complicated part of the schedule to a small group that rotates.

What happened to the numbers

I expected customers to pay at least a small price for our extra day off. As far as we can measure, they did not. Median first response time fell from 3.4 hours to 2.9 hours. Customer satisfaction rose from 4.3 to 4.5 out of 5.

On the team side, the change was larger. Annual attrition fell from 21 percent to 9 percent. Sick days fell by 31 percent. Those are the two numbers I care about most, because they are about people staying, and staying well.

I want to be careful about what this proves. We did not run a controlled experiment, and a lot can change in eighteen months. But the response time keeps nagging at me, in a good way. I had assumed fewer days would mean slower answers. My best guess is that a rested team simply works the queue with more focus. That is a guess, not a finding, and I would love to hear from anyone who has measured it properly.

What got harder

Not everything improved, and pretending otherwise would make this post useless to anyone thinking of trying it.

Thursday evenings became our weak spot. Everything still open at the end of the day has to be handed to the Friday group, and early on those handoffs were not always clean. A missing note, or a customer history that lived in one person’s head, could leave a ticket stalled. We have improved, but handoffs still need deliberate attention every week.

Two of our enterprise customers wanted guaranteed Friday phone support, which a four-person rotation could not promise on its own. Rather than drop the schedule or let those customers down, we hired 2 more people to cover it. That was a real cost, and anyone copying us should plan for something similar.

And a few people found nine-hour days tiring in winter. A long weekend does not erase a draining Wednesday. We have not fully solved this one. Mostly we talk about it openly and keep checking in.

If you try it

If you are considering a four-day week for your own support team, here is what I would tell you over coffee.

  1. Pilot for at least six months.The first weeks run on novelty, and the first winter brings fatigue. You need time to see past both before the numbers mean anything.
  2. Write the coverage rules before the first week.Decide who answers on Friday, when they take their day back, what happens when someone on rotation is sick, and how Thursday handoffs work. Rules written in a hurry after something breaks are rarely your best ones.
  3. Measure customer metrics alongside team morale.Either one alone will mislead you. A happy team with slipping response times is not a success, and neither is a fast queue staffed by people counting the days until they leave.

Eighteen months in, I am still curious about what we will learn next. But I no longer wonder whether it was worth trying.
