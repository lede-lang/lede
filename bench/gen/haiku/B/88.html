What Eighteen Months of Four-Day Weeks Did to Our Support Team

Priscilla Adeyemi-Koh, head of customer support

When we started, I had a long list of reasons to be nervous about a four-day week, and I was not shy about listing them. We ran a support team of twenty-three people, with a queue that never fully emptied and a habit of quietly wearing people down. Something had to change, so we tried one. This is an honest account of what happened.

Why we tried it

Our support team has twenty-three people. Like most support teams, we spent years running hard to keep up with a queue that never fully emptied. In 2024 our annual attrition was 21 percent, and when we asked people why they were leaving, burnout was the reason we heard most. We were not losing people because of pay or because they disliked the product. They were tired, and tired people do not stay.

So when the idea of a four-day week came up, I was skeptical, and I said so. I also wanted to know what we would lose. A support team that is closed on Friday has to answer a simple question: who picks up the phone?

How the schedule works

We started a pilot on April 7, 2025. Everyone works Monday to Thursday, nine hours a day, which comes to 36 hours a week at full pay. Friday coverage comes from a rotating on-call group of four people. Each person in that group works one Friday and takes the Monday after it off instead, so their hours still come out even.

The coverage rules were the most important part of the whole thing. We wrote them down before the first week, not after. Who is on call, how handoffs work, what happens when someone is sick, and who decides when a Friday is too busy to cover with four people. Most of the arguments we might have had in week three were settled in a document in week zero.

The on-call rota is posted a month ahead, and anyone can swap a shift with a colleague as long as coverage stays complete. Swaps turned out to be rare, which surprised me. I had expected them to be the thing everyone argued about.

What happened to the numbers

The pilot ended on October 2, 2026, when we decided to make the schedule permanent. We tracked customer numbers as carefully as team numbers, because a happier team that serves customers worse is not a success.

Median first response time fell from 3.4 hours to 2.9 hours. Customer satisfaction rose from 4.3 to 4.5 out of 5. Annual attrition fell from 21 percent to 9 percent. Sick days fell by 31 percent.

I would not claim the schedule caused every one of those changes. A team that is less tired answers faster, and a team that is less sick has more people on the phones on a given day. But the direction was consistent across every measure we watched, and that is what convinced me. We also looked at the numbers for each season separately, and the pattern held.

What got harder

It was not all good news. Thursday evenings became the hardest part of the week. Work that started on Thursday had to be handed off cleanly to whoever was on call on Friday, and in the first months a few handoffs were rough. We fixed most of that with a shared handoff note that everyone fills in before they log off. The note has a field for anything the Friday team needs to know about a customer, and it is the single most useful thing we added.

Two enterprise customers asked for guaranteed phone support on Fridays. We understood why, and we did not want to say no. So we hired two more people to cover that specific need. It was an honest cost, and I would rather say so than pretend the schedule was free.

Some people also found nine-hour days tiring in winter, when the light goes early and the days feel long. We have talked about this openly, and I do not think we have fully solved it yet.

If you try it

If you are thinking about a four-day week for your own team, here is what I would tell you.

Pilot for at least six months. Our first few weeks were noisy, and it took a while before anyone trusted the new rhythm. A shorter test will tell you about the novelty, not the change.

Write the coverage rules before the first week. Decide who covers what, how handoffs work, and what happens when something goes wrong. You will be tempted to improvise. Resist it.

Measure customer metrics alongside team morale. It is easy to celebrate a happier team and miss what is happening to customers. We watched both, and we would not have been able to make the decision with only one of them.

Eighteen months in, I am glad we tried it. The team stays together longer now, and our customers are still getting answers faster than before. That was the outcome we were after.
