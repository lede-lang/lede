What Eighteen Months of Four-Day Weeks Did to Our Support Team

By Priscilla Adeyemi-Koh, head of customer support

Why we tried it

In 2024, 21 percent of my support team left. We are 23 people, so that was about five colleagues in a single year, and when I read through why they went, one reason sat at the top of the list. Burnout was the reason people gave most often.

I did not want to believe it at first. Support work is hard in ways that are easy to wave away. The queue never really empties, the people writing to us are rarely writing on a good day, and the job asks you to be patient, accurate and kind for hours at a time. But waving it away was costing us a fifth of the team every year, along with everything those people knew about our maps, our customers and our odd corner cases.

So I asked a question I was slightly embarrassed to say out loud: what if we gave people one more day to recover and kept paying them the same? The pilot began on 7 April 2025. On 2 October 2026 we decided to make it permanent. Eighteen months is long enough that, somewhere around month nine, I stopped thinking of it as an experiment.

How the schedule works

Everyone works Monday to Thursday, nine hours a day. That is 36 hours a week, at full pay. Nobody took a pay cut, and I think that matters more than anything else in this essay. A four-day week that is quietly a pay cut with better branding is a different thing, and people can tell.

Friday is the part everyone asks about. Customers still write to us on Fridays, so we keep a rotating on-call group of four people. Whoever is in the group works Friday and takes Monday off instead. They still get a three-day weekend, just a shifted one, and because the group rotates, nobody is stuck holding Fridays for good.

On paper it is a small change. In practice it reshaped how we think about the whole week.

What happened to the numbers

I expected to defend the pilot with soft arguments about morale. The numbers did most of the work for me.

Median first response time fell from 3.4 hours to 2.9 hours. I would have bet money it would get worse, since we have one fewer day on the clock. Customer satisfaction rose from 4.3 to 4.5 out of 5. Annual attrition fell from 21 percent to 9 percent, which on a team of 23 is the difference between losing about five people and losing about two. Sick days fell by 31 percent.

I should be careful. This is one team at one company, and I cannot prove the schedule caused every improvement.

My guess about response time is that rested people answer faster and with fewer false starts, but that is a guess, not a finding. What I can say is that nothing we measured got worse, and the things we cared about most got better.

What got harder

It was not all smooth, and I would rather tell you that now than have you find it out later.

Thursday evening handoffs were the first problem. Everything that used to drift into Friday now has to be settled before the weekend starts, and in the early weeks work could slip through the gap between the people leaving and the on-call group picking it up.

The second was customers. Two of our enterprise customers wanted guaranteed Friday phone support, and a rotating group was not quite the same promise. We chose to take them seriously rather than argue, and we hired two more people to cover it. That cost real money, and I count it as part of the true price of the schedule.

The third is smaller and more human. A few people found nine-hour days tiring in winter. A longer day is a trade, not a gift, and for some people the trade is worse than for others. I do not have a clean answer for them yet.

If you try it

If you are thinking about this for your own team, here is what I would tell you over coffee.

  1. Pilot for at least six months.Early weeks measure novelty. You need enough time to see a hard month, and to see whether the gains hold once the excitement fades.
  2. Write the coverage rules before the first week.Who is on call, who takes which day off, and what happens when someone is sick. Decide it on paper, not in the middle of a busy Friday.
  3. Measure customer metrics alongside team morale.Morale is pleasant to report, but customers decide whether the schedule is sustainable. We tracked both from the start.

Eighteen months in, I would make the same decision again.
